CETa Objective Connection

This day supports CETa electrical theory and practical circuit objectives by helping students compare series and parallel paths, predict circuit behavior, identify branches, and document simple measurements safely.

Instructor Teaching Notes

Students often memorize “series is one path, parallel is multiple paths” without understanding what that means for voltage, current, and troubleshooting. Keep the lesson visual and practical. Use simple low-voltage LED/resistor examples and require students to trace the actual path before discussing formulas.

Emphasize safe limits. Students should not add multiple low-value loads without current-limiting resistance or instructor approval. All work stays on approved DC training sources.

Expanded Student Explanation

In a series circuit, components are connected one after another in a single path. If the path opens anywhere, current stops through the entire path. In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same source points in separate branches. One branch can open while another branch still works.

This difference matters in troubleshooting. If every light in a simple series path goes out, the technician looks for one open point in the only path. If one branch of a parallel circuit fails while others work, the source may still be present and the fault may be isolated to that branch.

Demonstration Sequence

  1. Draw one series circuit and one parallel circuit using the same source and two loads.
  2. Trace the series path from source positive to source negative.
  3. Open one connection and ask what stops working.
  4. Trace each parallel branch separately.
  5. Open one branch and ask what remains powered.
  6. Measure or demonstrate voltage across the source and selected points if equipment is available.

Worked Example

Scenario: Two LEDs are supposed to operate. In Circuit A, both LEDs go out when one lead is removed. In Circuit B, one LED stays on when the other branch opens. Students identify Circuit A as series and Circuit B as parallel, then explain the evidence.

Hands-On Practice

Students compare instructor-approved series and parallel training circuits. They should build or inspect only with power removed, predict behavior, and then test with approval.

Common Mistakes and Coaching

  • Students may call side-by-side physical placement “parallel.” Correct by tracing electrical nodes, not physical location.
  • Students may forget current-limiting resistors for LEDs. Enforce safety inspection before power.
  • Students may assume one failed branch means the source is dead. Teach branch isolation logic.
  • Students may mix series and parallel language. Require path tracing before naming the circuit.

Documentation Expectations

Students submit two annotated diagrams, one series and one parallel, with predicted fault behavior, source voltage if measured, and a short troubleshooting note for an open component in each circuit.

Check for Understanding

  1. What makes a circuit series?
  2. What makes a circuit parallel?
  3. Why can one branch fail while another still works?
  4. Why is tracing by nodes more reliable than looking at physical layout?

Exit Ticket

Given a simple circuit drawing, label it series, parallel, or mixed, then write the evidence that supports your answer.

Pacing and Remediation

Use 25 minutes for concept review, 25 minutes for demonstration, 60 minutes for trace/build/compare practice, 25 minutes for measurement documentation, and the remaining time for checkoff, shutdown, and AAR. Students who confuse physical location with electrical connection should repeat node-labeling practice.
