CETa Objective Connection

This day supports CETa objectives in electrical theory, formulas, current, voltage, resistance, and practical circuit analysis. Students should use Ohm's Law as a technician prediction tool, not simply a worksheet formula.

Instructor Teaching Notes

Students often memorize V = I × R without understanding when to use it. Start with the relationship: for a fixed voltage, higher resistance means lower current; lower resistance means higher current. Then show how the formula gives a numerical prediction before a circuit is powered.

Keep math values beginner-friendly. Use batteries and LED current-limiting examples. Reinforce units every time: volts, amps, milliamps, ohms. Require students to write the known values, the formula, the substitution, the answer, and the unit.

Expanded Student Explanation

Ohm's Law describes the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance. It helps a technician predict what should happen in a circuit. If the prediction and actual measurement are very different, that difference is a troubleshooting clue.

In an LED circuit, the resistor limits current so the LED is not damaged. Without an appropriate resistor, too much current may flow. The resistor is not just an extra part; it is a protective part. A technician should be able to explain why it is there and how its value affects current.

Demonstration Sequence

  1. Write V = I × R, I = V ÷ R, and R = V ÷ I on the board.
  2. Use a 9 V source and a 1,000 ohm resistor as a simple example.
  3. Calculate current: I = 9 V ÷ 1,000 ohms = 0.009 A = 9 mA.
  4. Discuss why 9 mA is safer for an LED than a very high current.
  5. Show how changing resistance changes predicted current.
  6. Have students predict before measuring or energizing.

Common Mistakes and Coaching

  • Students may mix milliamps and amps. Require conversion before calculating.
  • Students may forget units. Mark answers incomplete without units.
  • Students may use total source voltage when an LED voltage drop should be considered. Introduce LED drop concept gently and keep examples simple.
  • Students may think brighter is always better. Connect brightness to current stress and component life.
  • Students may build first and calculate later. Require prediction first.

Safety Boundaries

No circuit is powered until the instructor confirms the source, polarity, resistor value, LED orientation, and wiring path. Students may not test by intentionally shorting a source, bypassing a resistor, or swapping resistors while power is connected.

Documentation Evidence

Students submit calculation work showing known values, formula selection, substitution, answer, unit, and observation. The instructor checks whether measured or observed behavior reasonably matches the prediction.

Check for Understanding

  1. Why does current decrease when resistance increases?
  2. Why must milliamps be converted to amps in Ohm's Law calculations?
  3. What can a mismatch between prediction and observation tell a technician?
  4. Why should the current-limiting resistor be in series with the LED?
  5. Why is bypassing the resistor unsafe?

Exit Ticket

Calculate the expected current for a 5 V source and a 470 ohm resistor. Show the formula, substitution, answer, and unit. Add one sentence explaining whether this is reasonable for an LED practice circuit.

Instructor Pacing and Remediation

Use 25 minutes for relationship review, 30 minutes for formula practice, 25 minutes for worked examples, 45 minutes for prediction/observation table, 20 minutes for documentation review, and the remainder for checkoff, shutdown, and AAR. Students who struggle should use a three-column “known/formula/answer” worksheet before returning to circuit work.
