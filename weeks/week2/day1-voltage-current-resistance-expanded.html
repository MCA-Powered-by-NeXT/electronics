CETa Objective Connection

This day supports CETa electrical theory objectives: voltage, current, resistance, source/load/path concepts, polarity awareness, safe low-voltage DC work, and beginner circuit interpretation. The goal is for students to explain what is happening in a circuit before they begin measuring or changing it.

Instructor Teaching Notes

Teach voltage, current, and resistance as a connected system rather than three isolated definitions. Students should understand that voltage is electrical pressure or potential difference, current is the movement of charge through a complete path, and resistance opposes current. Avoid moving too quickly into formulas before students can describe the physical meaning.

Use low-voltage DC examples only. Reinforce that the course does not include live AC measurement. When referencing AC, keep it conceptual: AC changes direction; DC has a defined polarity. All hands-on work remains with approved batteries, holders, training boards, and instructor-approved DC sources.

Expanded Student Explanation

A circuit needs a source, a path, and a load. The source provides voltage. The path gives current a route. The load uses electrical energy to do work, such as lighting an LED or heating a resistor. Resistance limits how much current flows. If the path is open, current cannot flow. If the path is shorted, current may bypass the intended load and create heat or damage.

Students should picture current as the result of a complete circuit. A battery sitting by itself has voltage, but current does not flow unless there is a connected path. This distinction matters because technicians often diagnose circuits by asking, “Is the source present? Is the path complete? Is the load connected correctly? Is there too much or too little resistance?”

Demonstration Sequence

  1. Display a battery, switch, resistor, and LED without connecting them.
  2. Ask students to identify the source, possible path, and possible load.
  3. Draw the circuit path on the board before building anything.
  4. Show open switch versus closed switch behavior conceptually.
  5. Show how incorrect polarity affects an LED without forcing trial-and-error.
  6. Have students predict what happens when resistance increases or decreases.

Worked Example

Scenario: An LED circuit does not light. Students should not immediately move wires. They should reason through the system:

  1. Is the source present and approved?
  2. Is the battery connected with correct polarity?
  3. Is the circuit path complete?
  4. Is the LED oriented correctly?
  5. Is there current-limiting resistance?
  6. Is any wire loose, damaged, or placed in the wrong row?

Hands-On Practice

Students complete a circuit reasoning worksheet before handling parts. They identify source, path, load, expected current direction, possible open points, and possible short points. The instructor approves the drawing before students build with physical components.

Common Mistakes and Coaching

  • Students may say voltage “flows.” Correct: current flows; voltage exists between two points.
  • Students may believe current is used up. Explain that energy is transferred to the load, but current path continuity still matters.
  • Students may ignore polarity. Reinforce battery positive/negative and LED anode/cathode awareness.
  • Students may treat a short as just “a mistake.” Explain heat, battery stress, and component damage.

Check for Understanding

  1. What is the difference between voltage and current?
  2. Why can a battery show voltage even when no load is connected?
  3. What makes a circuit open?
  4. Why is a short circuit unsafe?
  5. How does resistance affect current?

Exit Ticket

Draw a simple circuit and label the source, path, load, voltage polarity, and one possible open-circuit fault. Add one sentence explaining why the circuit is or is not safe to energize.

Instructor Pacing and Remediation

Use 25 minutes for concept introduction, 20 minutes for vocabulary, 25 minutes for demonstration, 45 minutes for circuit reasoning practice, 30 minutes for supervised build/prediction, and the remainder for documentation, checkoff, shutdown, and AAR. Students who cannot distinguish voltage from current should complete an additional source/path/load sorting activity before moving to Ohm’s Law.
