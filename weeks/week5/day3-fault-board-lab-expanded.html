CETa Objective Connection

This lab supports CETa readiness in troubleshooting, DMM use, continuity testing, wiring and connector inspection, schematic comparison, and technician documentation. Students practice applying the method from Day 2 to a realistic controlled fault board.

Instructor Teaching Notes

Set the expectation that the fault-board lab is not a race. The best student is not the first one finished; the best student is the one who can prove the fault with evidence, correct it safely, and document the result clearly.

Use only instructor-prepared low-voltage fault boards. Faults may include open wire, loose connector, reversed LED, wrong resistor value, blown fuse, incorrect switch connection, disconnected return path, or misplaced breadboard jumper.

Expanded Student Explanation

A fault board is a training circuit with one or more intentional problems. The purpose is to practice the technician process under controlled conditions. Students should begin by inspecting the board, comparing it to the schematic, identifying the expected operation, and writing a test plan. They should avoid moving wires until evidence supports the correction.

The correct repair is not complete until the circuit is retested and the repair ticket is complete. In professional work, a technician must be able to explain the problem, the evidence, the correction, and the verification result.

Lab Procedure

  1. Complete the daily safety brief and PPE/bench check.
  2. Receive an instructor-assigned fault board and matching schematic.
  3. Do not power the board until instructed.
  4. Write expected operation in the repair ticket.
  5. Inspect wiring, components, terminals, and breadboard placement.
  6. Plan the first three tests before touching probes.
  7. Perform approved measurements or continuity tests.
  8. Record evidence after each test.
  9. Correct only the confirmed fault.
  10. Retest and document verification.

Instructor Demonstration Sequence

  1. Model receiving a board and refusing to energize it until inspection is complete.
  2. Read the schematic and expected operation aloud.
  3. Identify safe test points.
  4. Demonstrate one continuity test with power removed.
  5. Show how the evidence points to an open connection.
  6. Correct the fault and record the retest result.

Documentation Expectations

Students submit a complete repair ticket with board ID, symptom, expected operation, safety state, test plan, readings/observations, confirmed fault, correction, retest result, and AAR note.

Common Mistakes and Coaching

  • Students may want to touch or wiggle wires before documenting. Stop them and require initial inspection notes.
  • Students may skip the schematic. Require schematic-to-board comparison.
  • Students may identify the fault but fail to prove it. Ask, “What evidence supports that conclusion?”
  • Students may fix the board but forget retesting. Require verification before signoff.

Check for Understanding

  1. Why is a fault-board lab not a race?
  2. What should be written before the first measurement?
  3. Why should the board stay de-energized during continuity testing?
  4. What makes the repair complete?

Exit Ticket

Students write one sentence each for symptom, evidence, fault, correction, and verification from their assigned board.

Pacing and Remediation

Use 15 minutes for safety/setup, 20 minutes for demo, 75 minutes for lab troubleshooting, 25 minutes for repair ticket completion, and remaining time for checkoff, cleanup, shutdown, and AAR. Students who guess must reset their ticket and repeat the test-plan step.
