CETa Objective Connection

This day supports CETa technician work procedures, test equipment use, circuit analysis, service documentation, and systematic fault isolation. Students should learn to move from symptom to evidence instead of guessing and randomly changing parts.

Instructor Teaching Notes

Begin by contrasting guessing with troubleshooting. Guessing asks, “What part should I replace?” Troubleshooting asks, “What do I know, what do I need to prove, and what test will safely narrow the fault?” Students should learn that changing multiple things at once destroys evidence and makes repair results unreliable.

Keep all troubleshooting on de-energized or low-voltage instructor-approved boards. Students must identify the circuit state and measurement plan before probing.

Expanded Student Explanation

A technician troubleshoots by following a process: identify the symptom, verify the complaint, check safety, review the expected operation, divide the circuit into sections, test one thing at a time, compare actual results to expected results, correct the fault, retest, and document the repair.

Good troubleshooting is evidence-based. If an LED does not light, a technician does not immediately replace the LED. The technician checks source voltage, polarity, circuit path, resistor placement, switch condition, continuity, and connection quality. Each test should answer a specific question.

Demonstration Sequence

  1. Show a simple non-working training circuit.
  2. Ask students to describe the symptom without naming a cause.
  3. Review expected operation from the schematic or drawing.
  4. Create a test plan: source check, path check, load check, control check.
  5. Perform one measurement or continuity check at a time.
  6. Record each result in a troubleshooting table.
  7. Correct only the confirmed fault, retest, and document verification.

Hands-On Practice

Students work in pairs on a controlled fault scenario. One student reads the expected operation and fills the repair ticket; the other performs approved tests. Partners switch roles for a second scenario.

Students may not move a wire or replace a part until the repair ticket shows evidence that supports the action.

Common Mistakes and Coaching

  • Students may guess based on the first visible issue. Require a written symptom and expected operation first.
  • Students may change multiple things at once. Allow only one correction before retest.
  • Students may skip source verification. Coach them to start with power/source/path basics.
  • Students may write “fixed it.” Require symptom, test, result, correction, and verification.

Documentation Expectations

Each student completes a repair ticket including symptom, expected operation, safety state, test plan, test results, confirmed fault, corrective action, retest result, and technician initials/date.

Check for Understanding

  1. Why is guessing different from troubleshooting?
  2. Why should expected operation be written before testing?
  3. Why should a technician change only one thing at a time?
  4. What makes a repair ticket complete?

Exit Ticket

Students write a three-step troubleshooting plan for an LED circuit that does not turn on. The plan must include source, path, and load checks.

Pacing and Remediation

Use 25 minutes for method introduction, 30 minutes for instructor demonstration, 60 minutes for paired troubleshooting, 25 minutes for repair-ticket review, and remaining time for checkoff, shutdown, and AAR. Students who guess should restart from symptom and expected operation before continuing.
