CETa Objective Connection

This day supports CETa readiness in basic electrical theory: source, load, conductor, open circuit, closed circuit, voltage, current, resistance, polarity, and schematic/circuit-path thinking. The goal is conceptual fluency before formal Ohm's Law practice in Week 2.

Instructor Teaching Notes

Students often memorize words like voltage and current without being able to reason through a circuit. Use this day to build a durable mental model. The model does not need to be mathematically complete yet; it needs to help students predict whether a simple circuit can operate and explain why.

Keep safety boundaries visible: all examples use approved low-voltage DC training circuits. No live AC measurement, outlet work, open power-supply work, or unknown energized systems.

Expanded Student Explanation

A circuit needs a complete path. Energy leaves the source, moves through the circuit path, is used by a load, and returns to the source. If the path is broken, the circuit is open and current does not flow. If a wire bypasses the load and creates a very low-resistance path, the circuit can become a short circuit.

Voltage can be introduced as electrical pressure or potential difference. Current is the movement of electric charge through a complete path. Resistance is opposition to current. These are not separate ideas; they interact. Students should be able to describe what each idea means before calculating with them.

Worked Example

Scenario: A battery, resistor, and LED are wired in series. The LED does not light. Students should reason through these questions:

  1. Is the source present and connected?
  2. Is the circuit path complete from positive to load to return?
  3. Is the LED polarity correct?
  4. Is the resistor connected in the path rather than bypassed?
  5. Is there a loose wire or open connection?

The correct technician behavior is to power down, inspect, change one thing, power up, observe, and document.

Instructor Demonstration Sequence

  1. Draw a simple battery-resistor-LED circuit on the board.
  2. Ask students to trace the path with their finger or pencil.
  3. Mark one open point and ask what changes.
  4. Draw a bypass wire around the LED and explain short/bypass behavior.
  5. Use a physical breadboard or simulation to show the same circuit.
  6. Have students identify source, load, conductors, return path, and control point.

Hands-On Practice

Students receive three circuit diagrams or screenshots. For each one, they label the source, load, path, possible fault, and expected behavior. They should not answer only “works” or “does not work.” They must explain the reason.

Common Mistakes and Coaching

  • Students may think current leaves the battery and disappears at the load. Reinforce the complete path.
  • Students may call every fault a short. Require them to distinguish open, short, reversed polarity, and loose connection.
  • Students may focus on component names but ignore connection points. Have them trace nodes and paths.
  • Students may assume a circuit is safe because it is small. Return to the low-voltage boundary and PPE expectations.

Documentation Expectations

Students create a circuit-path note for each example: “The circuit should/should not operate because...” Each note must include one electrical reason and one safety action.

Check for Understanding

  1. What makes a circuit complete?
  2. What is the difference between an open circuit and a short circuit?
  3. Why can resistance protect an LED?
  4. Why should a technician describe the fault type instead of saying “it is broken”?

Exit Ticket

Students draw a simple LED circuit and label source, load, current path, resistor, and return path. They write one sentence explaining what would happen if one wire were removed.

Instructor Pacing and Remediation

Spend 20 minutes reviewing safety and simulation from Day 2, 35 minutes building the circuit model, 25 minutes on open/short examples, 45 minutes on annotation practice, 20 minutes on student explanations, and the remainder on checkoff, shutdown, and AAR. If students struggle, remove math entirely and return to path tracing.
