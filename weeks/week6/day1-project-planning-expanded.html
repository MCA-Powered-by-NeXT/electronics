CETa Objective Connection

This day connects the final project to CETa technician work procedures: reading requirements, planning work, selecting approved components, documenting decisions, managing safety boundaries, and explaining expected operation before building. Students should treat the project plan as a technician work order, not just a classroom worksheet.

Instructor Teaching Notes

Frame the final project as a capstone demonstration of the habits developed across the course: safety, source control, component identification, schematic interpretation, DMM setup, continuity checks, troubleshooting, documentation, and professional communication. Students should not begin building until they can explain what they intend to build, how it should operate, what parts are needed, what could go wrong, and how they will verify success.

Keep the safety boundary explicit: approved low-voltage DC training circuits only. No wall outlets, building wiring, open power supplies, vehicle systems, charged capacitors, or unknown energized equipment are used.

Expanded Student Explanation

A project plan helps a technician avoid guesswork. Before a circuit is built, the technician should know the purpose of the circuit, the expected behavior, the source voltage, the components required, the wiring path, the test points, and the documentation evidence needed. A strong project plan prevents common errors such as missing current-limiting resistance, reversed polarity, unclear breadboard layout, and unverified assumptions.

Key Vocabulary

Demonstration Sequence

  1. Show a sample final-project plan with purpose, schematic, parts list, test points, and safety note.
  2. Model how to convert a desired function into a simple source-path-load-control plan.
  3. Demonstrate how to identify the source, load, current-limiting component, switch/control, and return path.
  4. Point out what evidence the final rubric will require.
  5. Show a weak project plan and have students identify missing information.

Hands-On Planning Task

Students create a final project planning packet before building. The packet must include a brief purpose statement, simple schematic or wiring diagram, parts list, safety boundary statement, predicted operation, planned test points, and documentation checklist.

Planning ElementRequired Evidence
PurposeOne clear sentence explaining the circuit function.
Parts listValues and quantities for all components.
Safety planApproved source, PPE, power-off wiring rule, and shutdown plan.
VerificationAt least two measurements or checks that prove correct operation.

Common Mistakes and Coaching

  • Students may skip planning because the circuit seems simple. Require signoff before build.
  • Students may list parts without values. Require resistor values, source voltage, and component orientation where relevant.
  • Students may define success as “it works.” Require measurable evidence.
  • Students may plan to troubleshoot by trial and error. Require expected-operation language first.

Check for Understanding

  1. Why must a final project include planned test points?
  2. What is the difference between a parts list and a design intent?
  3. Why is measurable evidence better than saying “it works”?
  4. What safety boundary applies to every final project?

Exit Ticket

Students submit their project purpose, parts list, source voltage, two test points, and one predicted fault risk for instructor review.

Pacing and Remediation

Use 20 minutes for capstone expectations, 25 minutes for sample plan review, 60 minutes for student planning, 30 minutes for instructor conferences, and remaining time for documentation cleanup, shutdown, and AAR. Students with weak plans should revise before building on Day 2.
